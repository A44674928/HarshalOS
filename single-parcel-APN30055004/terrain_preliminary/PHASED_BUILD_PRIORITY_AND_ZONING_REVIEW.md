## 1. Contour mapping audit — important hold

The files named `APN30055004_1ft_contours_UTM12N.dxf` and `APN30055004_2ft_contours_UTM12N.dxf` are two contour-interval exports of the same modeled surface. They are not independent survey products.

The repository's terrain package expressly states that:
- the current bare-earth surface and contours are synthetic placeholders awaiting replacement with a real USGS 3DEP 1-meter DEM;
- a parcel proxy/approximately 180 m offset was used in the package's coordinate setup;
- the modeled bench elevations and slopes are synthetic-model outputs, not field-verified values; and
- an Arizona Registered Land Surveyor's ALTA/NSPS and one-foot topographic survey is still required before a sealed site plan or construction siting.

**Result:** The contour mapping is not approved for building placement. In particular, do not rely on it for a Phase 1 footprint, Phase 2 footprint, setbacks, finished-floor elevation, driveway grades, emergency access, drainage arrows, retaining walls, well location, septic field, reserve area, or disturbance calculations. The existing $200,000 budget annotation remains a project-level planning note, not a surveyed boundary or a cost estimate.

### Required mapping replacement

1. Obtain the actual USGS 3DEP 1-meter bare-earth GeoTIFF covering the legal parcel, with metadata and horizontal/vertical datum confirmed.
2. Obtain an AZ RLS boundary/ALTA-NSPS and one-foot topo survey tied to NAVD88, including monuments, easements, right-of-way, utilities, washes, and observed improvements.
3. Rebuild the 1-ft/2-ft contours from the actual elevation source; transform them into the County-required coordinate basis only after the surveyor/civil engineer confirms the basis.
4. Add recorded access/easement geometry, required setbacks, wildfire access/turnaround, well and sanitary protection, primary and reserve wastewater areas, drainage/floodplain, and protected trees.
5. Only then place and dimension the two building envelopes and compare cut/fill, snow storage, solar exposure, drainage, and utility trenching.

## 2. Priority 1 — fire-watch-style cabin / future ADU concept

### Architectural direction

A compact two-story cabin with the visual character of a historic fire-watch station, translated into a conventional, grounded residential building:
- simple rectangular footprint, approximately 16 ft × 20 ft as a test-fit starting point only;
- two compact levels, with total livable area designed not to exceed 600 sq ft unless the County confirms a larger entitlement;
- target overall height approximately 28–30 ft, leaving margin below the AR-zone 35-ft maximum; final height is measured and reviewed under the County's definitions and building code;
- steep, engineered metal roof; no rooftop deck, tall stilts, or unsupported lookout platform;
- upper-level observation/sitting room with controlled views and a modest balcony only if lot coverage, snow, guards, egress, and structural review allow;
- enclosed, code-compliant stair and independently code-compliant egress from sleeping areas;
- compact first-floor living/kitchen/bath/service core; upper level for sleeping/quiet work;
- one kitchen maximum if approved as an ADU;
- frost-protected foundation selected after geotechnical investigation; avoid assuming shallow piers or a slab is suitable before soils and frost criteria are known.

The 16 ft × 20 ft footprint and 28–30 ft height are concept targets, not dimensions validated against the synthetic contours. The County's ADU "livable area" definition and treatment of stairs, lofts, balconies, and floor-area calculations must be confirmed before freezing the plan.

### Cold-climate, water, and fire design

- Prioritize a continuous water-control layer, drained rainscreen, robust flashing, protected penetrations, durable roof edges, and controlled snow shedding. Waterproofing is a primary design system.
- Use a steep roof form engineered for the parcel-specific ground snow load, drifting, sliding snow, wind, and seismic criteria—not a generic Flagstaff or 60-psf assumption.
- Use a high-performance envelope, cold-climate heat pump, compact all-electric equipment where feasible, and a solar-ready roof/electrical panel.
- Treat roof runoff and snowmelt as a designed collection system with freeze protection, first-flush/filtration as appropriate, overflow routed to an approved on-parcel drainage solution, and potable/non-potable separation. Do not count collected rainwater as an approved potable supply until the County's adopted potable rainwater code and health requirements are satisfied.
- Use ignition-resistant exterior detailing, ember-resistant vents, a Class A roof assembly, screened/maintainable gutters, and defensible-space planning coordinated with the applicable fire district and current County wildfire requirements.
- Avoid exterior lighting that spills upward or beyond the parcel; design to the current County dark-sky rules and verify the parcel's lighting zone.

### Legal sequencing gate — do not assume this is already an ADU

The County's current ADU materials describe an ADU as a second, complete living space that is secondary to the main home. The zoning ordinance's ADU section governs attached and detached accessory units. Because the current project record does not establish an existing principal dwelling on this parcel, **a detached habitable ADU-first permit path is not confirmed**.

Before treating Priority 1 as an ADU, obtain a written Planning and Zoning interpretation answering:
1. Can a detached ADU be permitted and receive a certificate of occupancy before a principal dwelling exists on the parcel?
2. If not, can the Priority 1 structure be permitted first as a non-habitable accessory lookout/work structure, then lawfully converted to an ADU after the Phase 2 principal house is permitted and built?
3. If Phase 1 is instead permitted as the initial primary dwelling, can the County later approve the Phase 2 larger residence as the principal dwelling and reclassify Phase 1 as its detached ADU? What approvals and sequencing conditions apply?
4. Does the proposed two-story fire-watch aesthetic trigger any additional tower/height, fire-access, structural, or use review beyond a conventional two-story dwelling?
5. How will the County calculate livable area for the 600-sq-ft allowance and 75%-of-principal-dwelling cap?

Do not occupy a non-habitable accessory structure as a residence. Do not use a "fire watch" label to avoid dwelling, building, or fire-code review. The County's answer controls the permitted sequence.

## 3. Phase 2 — principal main-house concept

### Massing and organization

Use a compact, low-complexity forest-cabin residence, initially test-fit in the **1,200–1,600 sq ft** range, subject to the owner's room brief, affordability, and verified site constraints. This is a planning range, not a selected floor area.

Recommended organization:
- a central kitchen / dining / living room as the shared core;
- a quiet owner-bedroom zone separated from the public/common area;
- one or two flexible rooms for guests, work, or future needs, without pre-assigning occupancy;
- one or two bathrooms sized to the final household and code review;
- a compact laundry, mechanical, pantry, and mudroom/service spine;
- a covered entry and weather-protected outdoor sitting area;
- simple roof geometry and repeated window/door sizes to control cost and reduce waterproofing complexity.

Prefer a compact one-story or 1½-story form over a complicated multi-wing roof until the actual topography, snow loads, wildfire access, and cost model are known. A bermed element remains an option only after geotechnical, waterproofing, drainage, radon, and cost review.

### Phase 2 coordination

Reserve the main-house envelope and infrastructure corridors before building Phase 1. The eventual site plan must coordinate:
- the principal-house footprint and its full required yards;
- the detached ADU separation limit, if Phase 1 becomes an ADU;
- well and sanitary protection areas;
- primary and 100% reserve wastewater areas;
- driveway, fire-apparatus access, turnaround, snow storage, and construction staging;
- roof-water capture, cisterns, overflow, and on-parcel stormwater retention;
- solar orientation and shading from both buildings;
- utility trenches, service panels, future meter options, and maintainable access;
- protected trees and a disturbance budget.

Do not design Phase 1 as a standalone solution that blocks the Phase 2 main-house envelope, septic reserve, emergency access, or solar exposure.
