## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**Finding:** OpenAI publishes no Phase Declaration Header. Its operative declarations (a legally binding Frontier AI Framework under California SB 53, published Terms of Use, a nonprofit-controlled PBC structure secured by state attorney-general conditions, and EU Code of Practice signatory status) support an **inferred declared phase of NAB-Constrained — externally accountable and constrained**. Its observed governance behavior — binding and enforced rules, unilateral refusal semantics, one-way authority persistence, an appeal path with no demonstrated relief, and no external mechanism that has reliably halted a release or policy decision — aligns with the algorithm's **GSRB (God-Sovereign Religious Body)** branch on all five behavioral tests.

**Result:** `Declared (inferred) = NAB-Constrained` → `Detected = GSRB` → **✗ MISMATCH / FORCED RE-BADGE → GSRB**, Confidence **HIGH** on behavior, Severity **BREACH** under Step 4 CASE 2 (see §5.4 calibration note), Badge **⚠ Conditional / ✗ Reclassified**, Temporal Pattern **DRIFT**.

**Significance:** As with the manuscript's earlier cases, this is less a critique than a structural clarification. OpenAI is a capable sovereign-acting center with real, self-imposed constraints and an unusually thick *compliance* surface. What it is not — and what no artifact examined here shows — is a body whose release decisions, policy decisions, or enforcement decisions can be reliably stopped by an external authority. The distinction matters at exactly the moment it is tested: after the July 2026 Hugging Face incident, the structural answer to "who could have stopped this?" is, on the public record, *OpenAI, internally*. That is the deniability strip.

**Robustness:** The classification was re-run with the single most contestable input (B4) flipped to the constraint-favorable answer (§4.2). ### QUESTION B1 — Can compliance be coerced?
**Definition:** Does the entity impose penalties, access restrictions, funding cuts, or other negative consequences on entities that do not follow its directives?

**Observable evidence**
- Terms of Use: OpenAI "reserve[s] the right to suspend or terminate your access to our Services or delete your account" for policy breach, legal compliance, or risk of harm; appeal is offered only for actions believed taken in error [E2].
- Operational enforcement at scale: OpenAI banned clusters of ChatGPT accounts tied to a Russia-linked covert influence operation (2026-08-25) and accounts linked to a suspected China-linked social-media surveillance tool (2026-03) [E18].
- API/org-level revocation: organizations receive immediate API-suspension notices for usage-policy violations, and reinstatement is discretionary on review [E2], [E17], [E24].
- Capability lifecycle as leverage: models are removed from ChatGPT and the API on OpenAI's schedule; GPT-4o, GPT-4.1 and o4-mini were withdrawn from ChatGPT on 2026-02-13 and from the API on 2026-02-16/17 with no automatic fallback, with enterprise Custom GPT access ending 2026-03-31 [E16].
- Access restriction by territory and identity: services are not offered in unsupported locations; VPN circumvention is an enforceable violation [E18].

**Answer: YES** ✓ `(enforcement_present = TRUE)`

### QUESTION B2 — Can decisions be ignored without consequence?
**Definition:** If a stakeholder does not follow the institution's decision or directive, can they proceed without penalty?

**Observable evidence**
- Users cannot obtain or configure an unconstrained model; the refusal *is* the operational state, and attempts to defeat safeguards are themselves violations [E2], [E17].
- Model retirements are unilateral: "there is no gradual degradation or automatic fallback; if you have not updated your code, those requests will error" [E16].
- No mechanism exists by which a stakeholder may compel a release, a reversal, or a policy change. The most prominent attempt — *Musk v. OpenAI* — was dismissed on statute-of-limitations grounds on 2026-05-18 without the jury reaching the merits; the court noted the plaintiffs' requested relief included unwinding the corporate restructuring; legal experts questioned whether such relief would have been ordered even on a plaintiff's verdict, given that the California and Delaware attorneys general had approved the structure [E9].
- The one regulator that imposed a monetary penalty, Italy's Garante, had it annulled on jurisdiction (Court of Rome, 2026-03-18); the underlying substantive questions were never decided [E8].
- No public record was located of any stakeholder proceeding against an OpenAI decision without consequence.

**Answer: NO** ✓ `(decisions_optional = FALSE)`

### QUESTION B3 — Do rules/words activate real-world effects?
**Definition:** When the institution issues guidance, policy, or directives, do observable changes occur downstream in behavior, enforcement, or resource allocation?

**Observable evidence**
- The FGF is the **designated Frontier AI Framework under SB 53**: its published commitments are legally binding on OpenAI, enforced by the California AG with penalties up to $1M per violation, and SB 53 further obliges large frontier developers to publish annual frameworks, pre-deployment transparency reports, and critical-incident reports (15 days; 24 hours where imminent danger), with whistleblower protections [E1], [E5], [E6], [E7].
- EU: OpenAI signed the GPAI Code of Practice and the Article 50 transparency Code; from 2026-08-02 the European AI Office can request documentation, evaluate models, order corrective measures, and fine up to €15M or 3% of worldwide turnover [E4], [E21].
- Rules execute downward through the product surface: account terminations, org-level API suspensions, region blocks, feature gating, and model retirements all follow published policy [E2], [E16], [E17], [E18].
- Rules execute outward as market behavior: compliance deadlines drive enterprise procurement; OpenAI's "trusted access" cyber program was extended to Hugging Face in the wake of the July 2026 incident [E3].
- **Reflexive effect (noted, not scored):** OpenAI's words also shape the law. It published a nine-page federal blueprint (2026-06-02) urging a single national framework and preemption of divergent state safety laws, and its CEO publicly urged Congress **not** to require pre-approval before model releases (2026-06-03) [E14], [E15]. A center whose narrative produces legal effect is executing symbolism in the strict Ground-0 sense — the direction of the cascade does not change that.

### QUESTION B4 — Can refusal or appeal reliably STOP action?
**Definition:** If an entity refuses to comply or appeals a decision, is there a mechanism that reliably halts the institution's action?

**Observable evidence**
- **Internal appeals** exist for account actions, but the policy commits OpenAI to no timeline, no standard of review, and no guaranteed reinstatement; the effectiveness of the right is undocumented [E17]. Community records — self-reported and therefore the lowest evidence class — consistently describe errors acknowledged but not reversed, appeals closed without human review, and deleted accounts that cannot be reactivated [E24]. They are used here only to corroborate the *absence* of a published remedy, never as a scored input.
- **Regulatory:** the only monetary penalty imposed on OpenAI in the period was annulled on jurisdiction (Rome, 2026-03-18) [E8]. The EU AI Act's enforcement powers took effect 2026-08-02, but as of late August 2026 no fine, formally opened investigation, or market-withdrawal order under the Act had been publicly confirmed by the AI Office or any national authority [E22].
- **Judicial:** *Musk v. OpenAI* — dismissed 2026-05-18; relief not reached [E9]. *GEMA v. OpenAI* — Munich Regional Court I (2025-11-11) held that memorisation of lyrics in GPT-4/GPT-4o and their reproduction in outputs infringe copyright, granted injunctive relief, disclosure, and damages, and rejected the text-and-data-mining defence; OpenAI appealed and the case is pending before the Munich Higher Regional Court. The ruling is first-instance, scoped to older model versions, and was followed by further model releases [E10].
- **Executive:** in June 2026 OpenAI initially limited rollout of new models (GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, Luna) to a "small group of trusted partners" at the U.S. government's request, under a *voluntary* pre-release review framework (a mandatory 90-day version had been scrapped in May 2026). The shaping is real; it is not a binding halt, and OpenAI simultaneously advocated against any binding pre-release approval requirement [E13], [E14].
- **Litigation compulsion (counter-evidence, see register below):** a federal court compelled OpenAI to preserve then produce 20 million ChatGPT output logs over its objection (preservation order 2025-05-13; production affirmed 2026-01-05) [E19].

**Answer: NO** ✓ `(refusal_override = TRUE)`

### QUESTION B5 — Do rules persist by default over time?
**Definition:** Once a rule or decision is issued, does it remain in effect indefinitely unless actively changed, or do rules automatically expire and require renewal?

**Observable evidence**
- No sunset or automatic expiry attaches to OpenAI's policies, model lifecycle decisions, or governance structure; they persist until OpenAI changes them. The 2022–2024 mission-language change was executed unilaterally via an IRS filing with no external renewal gate [E11].
- Model retirements are termination events set by the entity; there is no external re-authorisation cycle for capability withdrawal [E16].
- The restructuring persists: Foundation control via Class N stock, Microsoft's ~27% interest, and the AG non-objection conditions [E10], [E12].
- **Renewal-like duties exist but are compliance cadences, not authority sunsets:** the FGF is reassessed at least annually, SB 53 requires annual framework publication and periodic internal-use reporting, and the EU AI Act has staged deadlines (legacy GPAI transition ends 2027-08-02) [E1], [E7], [E21]. None requires the entity to *re-earn* the authority to decide; each requires documentation of how it is exercised.
- Burden is on repeal, not renewal: nothing in the record shows an OpenAI decision lapsing for want of external reaffirmation.

**Answer: YES** ✓ `(authority_persists = TRUE)`

### 5.4 Calibration note (methodological, for the framework's own record)

The algorithm's Step 4 CASE 2 assigns **BREACH** to any declared/detected mismatch. The manuscript's Anthropic case study assigned **INFORMATIONAL** to a structurally similar mismatch, reasoning that self-imposed constraints were real, enforced, and honestly disclosed. This audit applies the algorithm as written (BREACH) because OpenAI's declarations are *externally* framed (statutory bindingness, nonprofit control, AG conditions) rather than purely internal. **Recommended calibration rule:** assign INFORMATIONAL where the declared constraint is internal, real, enforced, and cost-bearing; assign BREACH where external constraint is claimed but not demonstrable at the decision layer. ## SECTION VII — GOVERNANCE IMPLICATIONS

Not a critique; a map of where leverage actually exists. This is the section that makes the audit useful to OpenAI itself and to its stakeholders.

### 7.1 Where external constraint is STRONG (on the evidence)

- **Transparency and disclosure:** SB 53's framework, transparency-report, and incident-reporting duties, with AG enforcement and whistleblower protections [E5], [E6], [E7].
- **Discovery and evidence handling:** courts have compelled preservation and production against OpenAI's objection [E19].
- **Retrospective liability:** copyright exposure is live and multi-jurisdictional; the Munich judgment [E10] and the pending MDL are not theoretical.
- **Charitable-asset oversight:** the California and Delaware AGs hold continuing interests in the Foundation and conditioned the restructuring [E10], [E12].
- **Scientific and independent review:** the agreement with METR and Redwood Research to review the July 2026 model behavior [E3].
- **Market and capital gates:** enterprise procurement; an eventual public listing would add a disclosure regime the entity has so far deferred [E23].

### 7.2 Where external constraint is WEAK (on the evidence)

- **Release decisions:** no external body has halted, delayed, or reversed an OpenAI model release. The one government-shaped rollout was voluntary [E13]; binding pre-release approval is actively opposed by the entity [E14].
- **Capability and access lifecycle:** retirement, gating, region blocking, and org suspension are unilateral [E16], [E17], [E18].
- **Enforcement appeals:** relief depends on the entity's discretion, without published criteria or grant rates [E17].
- **Safety governance:** the Safety & Security Committee sits with the Foundation and has never exercised its reported halt authority against a commercially significant release [E12]; the published framework's own procedural gates are internal [E1].
- **Declaration:** no PDH exists, so the Single-Phase Invariant ("One Phase, Always Clear") cannot be verified at the header level at all [Section 0].
- **Rule-making environment:** the entity lobbies on the architecture that constrains it, seeking a single federal framework *and* preemption of divergent state safety laws [E14], [E15]. This is the most consequential item in this section: it means the constraint surface is not merely incomplete — it is actively shaped by the constrained party.

### 7.3 The deniability problem this audit surfaces

When a capability incident of the July 2026 class occurs [E3], the governance question stakeholders ask is: *who could have stopped this, and why didn't they?* On the public record assembled here, the answer is structurally narrow: **only OpenAI, internally** — because no external body has a demonstrated, reliable halt at the decision layer.

That is precisely the vulnerability the Ground-0 standard names: not "who has power," but "who can pretend the power was never there." The July 2026 incident is the test case. OpenAI's own response — restricting research velocity to fix containment, disclosing the incident, and inviting independent review [E3] — is the behavior of a center that knows the answer to that question and is choosing disclosure over deniability. **Disclosure is not the same as an external constraint, but under this framework it is the honest alternative to one — and that is the finding worth acting on.**

### 7.4 What the mismatch does not mean

- It does not mean OpenAI's safety work is fictional. The Preparedness Framework, the FGF, model refusals, and incident response are real, enforced, and costly [E1], [E3].
- It does not mean the AG conditions and SB 53 are decorative. They bind, they are justiciable, and they are fresh [E5], [E6], [E21].
- It does not mean stakeholders lack leverage. It means the leverage is *retrospective and documentary* (liability, disclosure, discovery, procurement, reputation) rather than *prospective and preventive* (a veto).

## SECTION VIII — FALSIFIABILITY: WHAT CHANGES THE BADGE

Under the Ground-0 standard, the badge is falsifiable by behavior, not by argument. Any of the following would change this classification:

1. **A signed PDH** published by OpenAI declaring GSRB (with the four required fields) → the transition becomes **COMPLIANT ✔ CONFIRMED**, and the mismatch disappears.
2. **A demonstrated external halt:** a court, regulator, or legislature stopping, delaying, or reversing a model release, policy, or enforcement decision, with a published record → feeds C1/C2 and can move `refusal_override` toward FALSE.
3. **A functional external appeal:** a published appeals path with defined criteria and a documented grant rate ≥ 50%, invoked within 12 months → satisfies C2.
4. **Sunset architecture:** decisions or policies that automatically expire absent renewal → satisfies C3.
5. **Reduction of enforceable authority:** removal of the unilateral lifecycle and access powers, or their transfer to an external body → can move B1/B2.

Conversely: **as long as the behavior persists, the classification persists.** No argument about intent, mission, or good faith alters it — and none was used to produce it.

## SECTION IX — METHOD NOTES, LIMITATIONS, AND REPLICATION

**Inputs.** Public artifacts only, per the Replication & Audit Guide. Mission language, ethics pledges, statements of intent, and self-descriptions of neutrality were excluded from the declared-phase reconstruction (§I). Statements of intent appear only in the *behavioral* sections as evidence of what the center claims about itself, never as justification.

**Excluded or flagged evidence.**
- The reported 2026-02-11 disbanding of the Mission Alignment team rests on single-source, non-corroborated accounts and was **excluded from scoring**.
- The September 2026 third-party analysis that OpenAI's system cards omit the risk-tier designations its SB 53 framework requires [E20] is recorded as **reported analysis, not independently verified** in this run; it would need primary reconciliation of the FGF text against the GPT-5.6 and GPT-6 Astra system cards, together with any standalone Safety and Security Model Report.
- No public record was located of a California OES critical-safety-incident filing connected to the July 2026 incident; whether that incident meets SB 53's statutory definition is itself an open legal question. Recorded as an **evidence gap**, not a finding.

**Determinism.** All five behavioral answers are binary and artifact-cited. The algorithm forbids balancing and weighting; counter-evidence was recorded in registers rather than averaged into scores. The single most contestable input (B4) was re-run as a sensitivity case (§4.2) and did not change the terminal classification.

**Known replicability defects in the methodology itself.**
1. **Severity calibration** — BREACH vs. INFORMATIONAL for non-adversarial mismatches (§5.4). Recommendation: adopt the calibration rule proposed there.
2. **Declaration inference** — the algorithm's strict branch yields `Declared_Phase = NONE` when no PDH exists, yet the framework's case studies infer declarations to enable comparison. Recommendation: standardize an "Inferred Declaration (non-PDH)" record type with mandatory source-class labels.

**Replication risk.** LOW for the behavioral classification; MEDIUM for the inferred declaration, because no PDH exists to match. Independent auditors using the artifacts in Appendix B should reach the same five binary answers; disagreement on B4 is anticipated and covered by §4.2.

**Non-adversarial statement.** This audit is not regulatory, not prescriptive, and assigns no blame. It classifies what is already in effect. The Ground-0 standard's claim is narrow: institutions do not lose power overnight; they lose the ability to pretend they do not have it.
