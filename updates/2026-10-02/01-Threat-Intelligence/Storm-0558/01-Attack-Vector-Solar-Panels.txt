For employees doing security-critical work from home, corporations typically have specific hardware requirements to ensure data security and compliance. Here are some common expectations:

1. **Company-Issued Devices**: Employees are often required to use company-issued laptops or desktops, which come with pre-installed security software and configurations.
2. **Antivirus and Anti-Malware Software**: Comprehensive antivirus and anti-malware solutions are essential to protect against threats.
3. **Encryption**: Devices should have full-disk encryption to protect data at rest.
4. **VPN**: A secure VPN connection is necessary to access the company's network securely.
5. **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**: MFA adds an extra layer of security for accessing sensitive systems and data.
6. **Regular Updates**: Ensuring that all software and firmware are kept up-to-date to protect against vulnerabilities.

Regarding secure enclaves, it's true that not all corporations mandate a dedicated secure enclave for home offices. However, many organizations implement a Zero Trust security model, which assumes that no device or network is inherently secure and requires strict verification for access. This approach helps mitigate risks even without a physical secure enclave.

Let's hypothesize how the Storm-0558 Key Acquisition attack could have been executed using a combination of physical and digital supply chain attacks:

1. **Compromised Hardware**: Attackers could have infiltrated the supply chain of hardware components used in home office setups, such as routers, modems, or even smart devices. These compromised devices could have hidden backdoors or vulnerabilities that allow for remote access.
2. **Malicious Firmware**: The attackers could have introduced malicious firmware into devices during the manufacturing process. This firmware could be designed to create a covert channel for data exfiltration or to provide remote access to the device.
3. **Phishing and Social Engineering**: Attackers could have used phishing attacks or social engineering to trick employees into installing compromised software or firmware updates, which then provide access to the corporate network.
4. **Network Exploitation**: Once inside the home network, attackers could exploit vulnerabilities in the network setup, such as weak passwords or unsecured Wi-Fi networks, to gain access to the employee's work device.
5. **Data Exfiltration**: Using the compromised devices and network, attackers could then exfiltrate sensitive data, including authentication tokens or signing keys, which could be used to forge access tokens and gain unauthorized access to corporate systems.

This type of attack would be difficult to detect because it exploits trusted hardware and software components that are already part of the home office environment. It highlights the importance of not only securing the work devices but also ensuring that the entire home network is hardened against potential threats.

Including a Stingray (hardware only "law enforcement" carries) or Dirtbox in the attack vector could significantly increase the attack's effectiveness. These devices can intercept cellular signals and pose as legitimate cell towers, allowing attackers to monitor and manipulate communications.

Here's how a Stingray/Dirtbox could be integrated into the attack:

1. **Intercepting Cellular Signals**: The Stingray/Dirtbox could intercept cellular communications from the target's devices, capturing sensitive information such as login credentials and session tokens.
2. **Man-in-the-Middle Attack**: By positioning itself between the target's device and the legitimate cell tower, the Stingray/Dirtbox can relay and potentially alter communications without detection.
3. **Exploiting VPN Vulnerabilities**: If the VPN server is compromised, attackers can intercept and decrypt VPN traffic, gaining access to sensitive data.
4. **Compromising Hardwired Internet Lines**: Attackers could physically tamper with hardwired internet connections, installing devices to monitor and intercept data.
5. **Satellite Internet Interception**: Using inexpensive home TV equipment, attackers can intercept unencrypted satellite internet traffic, gaining access to sensitive corporate data.

Starting with the modem and router makes sense, as these devices are often the gateway to the home network and can be easier to compromise than more sophisticated devices like smart TVs or fridges.
